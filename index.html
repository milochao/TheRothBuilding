The Roth Building

300 Homer Avenue, 1932 to now

scroll

1924-1932

In 1924, a doctor named Russel Lee opened a practice on Bryant Street with a colleague, Thomas Williams. Within six years, four more doctors joined them: Edward Roth, a surgeon, in 1925. Esther Clark, a pediatrician, in 1927, the only one between San Francisco and San Jose at the time. She was the sister of the architect who’d soon design their building, Birge Clark; one later account gets this backward and calls him hers. Blake Wilbur, trained at the Mayo Clinic, in 1930. By then there were six of them, and they called it the Palo Alto Clinic.

RLRussel V. A. Leeinternist · Bryant Street, 1924
Born 1895 in Spanish Fork, Utah, a minister’s son; he arrived in Palo Alto at sixteen with eight dollars, enrolled at Stanford, and took his M.D. there in 1920. He taught medicine at Stanford across four decades while directing the clinic he founded. In 1951, President Truman appointed him to the Commission on the Health Needs of the Nation, whose plan later became a basis for Medicare. He donated and sold the city the 1,400 acres that became Foothills Park, and founded Channing House, opened January 1964, with lifetime medical care from the clinic’s own doctors. The record disagrees about his children: five sons per one Stanford finding aid, four sons and a daughter per another, five sons and a daughter per his own grandson’s account, all of them physicians. He died at home in January 1982, at 86.

TWThomas Williamsthe first partner · 601 Bryant
The senior partner, and the reason there was a practice to join: Lee moved down from San Francisco in 1924 to join Dr. Williams’s office building at Bryant Street and Hamilton Avenue, 601 Bryant. The National Register nomination records him as the elder of the group, retired in 1929, the year before Blake Wilbur replaced him and three years before the Homer Avenue building opened. Beyond that, the open record goes quiet: no verifiable birth or death dates, education, or specialty survive online.

EREdward Rothsurgeon · joined 1925
Edward Frederick Roth, called Fritz. Born in Ukiah, Stanford M.D. 1920, Lee’s class, with further surgical training in Boston. He joined the practice in 1925 to cover surgery and obstetrics, later returned to his first love, orthopedics, and became Stanford’s team physician in the 1930s, a post he held the rest of his career. When the murals drew crowds in 1932, he threatened to have the walls whitewashed before he would move in. When the practice outgrew the building in 1961, the new building took Lee’s name and the original took his. He died in 1972.

ECEsther Clarkpediatrician · joined 1927
Daughter of a Stanford art professor and sister of the building’s architect. In 1927 she was the only pediatrician on the mid-Peninsula, and among the first women physicians in California. She practiced until 1972, founded the Children’s Health Council, and chaired a national pediatricians’ commission on child accident prevention. In 1944 she bought land in the hills that had been a 1930s airstrip; the city park on it now carries her name.
Sources: Wikipedia · City of Palo Alto
Portrait: None cleared; her Wikipedia article has no photograph. PAMF and PAHA materials are the likeliest holders.
BWBlake Wilbursurgeon · joined 1930
Blake Colburn Wilbur, 1901 to 1974. San Francisco-born son of Stanford’s president Ray Lyman Wilbur. Stanford Phi Beta Kappa in 1922, Harvard M.D. 1925, training at the Mayo Clinic, then home: he joined the group on January 1, 1930, replacing the retiring Williams, and became renowned as its surgeon. A clinical professor of surgery at Stanford for many years, he operated up to his death in 1974. Stanford named Blake Wilbur Drive for him the following year, and in 1993 opened an outpatient clinic under his name.
Sources: National Register nomination · Wikipedia
Portrait: None cleared. Stanford’s archives are the likeliest holder. Photos of his father circulate freely; they are not him.
BCBirge Clarkarchitect · designed 300 Homer
Stanford 1914, Columbia architecture 1917, and between the two a World War I observation-balloon pilot, shot down and awarded the Silver Star. Back home he became the architect Palo Alto is still made of: the Lou Henry Hoover House, the 1932 post office, the Lucie Stern Community Center, and his sister’s clinic at 300 Homer, in the Spanish Colonial Revival manner that earned him the name of Palo Alto’s best-loved architect. He died in 1989, at 96.
Sources: Wikipedia · Stanford exhibits
Portrait: None cleared; his Wikipedia article has no photograph. His papers and portraits sit in Stanford’s Birge M. Clark collection.

Clark built them a home in 1932, at 300 Homer Avenue, and brought in a muralist, Victor Arnautoff, to paint the walls. Arnautoff painted patients being examined, some of them partly undressed, and when the murals went up, the San Francisco Chronicle didn’t care for them. The clinic kept the murals anyway.

VAVictor Arnautoffmuralist · painted the walls, 1932
Born 1896 in Uspenovka, in the Russian Empire, now Ukraine. He learned fresco as Diego Rivera’s assistant in Mexico from 1929 to 1931, and the clinic walls were his first mural commission, painted in August 1932 for a practice where he had been a patient: four color frescoes of modern medicine at work, grisaille panels of older methods beneath, and medallions of Lister, Hippocrates, Pasteur and Roentgen. He painted Coit Tower’s City Life in 1934, taught art at Stanford from 1939 to 1963 per the National Register nomination, was questioned by HUAC for his politics, and after his wife’s death returned to the Soviet Union, dying in Leningrad in 1979. The frescoes joined the city’s public art collection in 2016 and were fully restored in 2026.
Sources: Wikipedia · National Register nomination · City staff report · The Almanac, Jul 2 2026 · Living New Deal
Portrait: A 1936 news photograph of Arnautoff with the artist Esther Bruton is listed as public domain on PICRYL and sits on Wikimedia Commons. Attach the file and it drops into this frame.

The doctors did well, especially Lee. He hired Esther Clark himself, and years later built a retirement home in town, Channing House, in 1964. There’s more to his story than that, but it doesn’t enter the record until decades later, in an unlikely place.

By 1955, state law required a name change: Palo Alto Medical Clinic. By 1961, the practice had outgrown 300 Homer and moved next door, to a building named for Lee. The old building got a new name too, after Edward Roth. It kept doing its job for four more decades.

2000-2009

In April of 2000, the city bought the building and its third of an acre, for somewhere between $1.9 and $2 million depending which year’s accounting you trust, and named it a local landmark the same year. Then it mostly sat there.

Ask when the museum itself started and you’ll get three different years, and the people who’d know keep giving different ones depending on when you ask them. City records point to 2003, when the city first signed something with a group calling itself the nascent Palo Alto History Museum. The nonprofit’s own telling, a few years on, put it at 2004, with a lease option that same year. By 2007, the group was pitching the idea publicly and applying to put the building on the National Register. A project like this rarely gets one clean birthday; more often it gets a string of moments where somebody finally put something in writing, and different people were standing in different rooms for different ones.

Carol Murden was standing in one of the earliest ones. She’d served on the city’s Historic Resources Board since 1996, chaired it for a year, and had belonged to Palo Alto Stanford Heritage since 1992. She co-wrote the application that eventually got the Roth Building onto the National Register. She died December 27, 2008. The nomination was dated the following year; the building didn’t reopen for another eighteen. It stood empty every year she worked on it.

CMCarol Murdenpreservationist
The Palo Alto Historical Association’s memorial gave her two words: historian, preservationist. She served on the boards of Palo Alto Stanford Heritage, PAHA (as corresponding secretary), and the Palo Alto History Museum itself, sat on the city’s Historic Resources Board, and joined the history committee of the Woman’s Club. PAST gave her its lifetime preservation award in 2002. With Beth Bunnenberg she applied successfully to put the Roth Building on the National Register; the listing came on June 21, 2010, a year and a half after her death.

2007

That same year, the basement almost ended it. A leak, traced to water damage from two old medical wings that had been torn off the building years earlier, drove repair costs high enough that city staff recommended Palo Alto simply stop paying for the place. The council overruled its own staff, voted unanimously for a forty-year lease at a dollar a year, and put another $150,000 toward the leak. Vice Mayor Larry Klein publicly opposed the expense anyway, pointing out the city had already spent $3.3 million. Another council member, Jack Morton, called the lease "a wonderful deal." A board member named Robin Robinson told the council the nonprofit had raised $2 million of the roughly $7 million it needed.

2010-2020

By 2010, there was a real fundraising strategy, built with a retired Stanford fundraiser named Dudley Kenworthy, and an early lead gift of $2 million from the Peery Family Foundation. The city’s own historian, Steve Staiger, made the case in a newspaper that year: nearly every other city in the county had some kind of history museum. Palo Alto didn’t. The estimate then was "more than $5 million." A board member, Margaret Maloney, described a planned exhibit on venture capital. The plan was to start renovation in May of 2011 and open in October of 2012.

SSSteve Staigerboard member · PAHA historian since 1984
Raised in Marin County, Steve Staiger came to the Peninsula in 1973 and spent 1977 to the early 2000s as a City of Palo Alto reference librarian. In 1984 he added a second job, unpaid historian for the Palo Alto Historical Association, keeper of the city-owned Guy Miller Archives, a post he still holds. He was pushing for a local history museum in the Roth Building from the beginning, and made the case in print in 2010: nearly every other city in the county had one, and Palo Alto didn’t. He now sits on the museum’s own board.

That target came and went, and so did most of the ones after it. By 2014, the price had grown to $7.2 million and the group was $2.4 million short. City staff told the council they believed the museum could be running by the fall of 2016. It wasn’t. By 2017, the number was $9.2 million, Rich Green was board president partway through a $20 million campaign, and the group had raised something like $5.7 million total, across private gifts, a sale of development rights, and city money. That December, the council granted a one-year lease extension, conditioned on raising $1.75 million within the year, voting 8 to 0 with one member absent. Vice Mayor Liz Kniss, who made the motion, noted the building had sat unused for eighteen years and parts of it weren’t sturdy anymore. A woman named Kimberley Wong, already married to board chair Nelson Ng, testified in favor that day.

RGRich Greenboard president · twelve years
An audio and video designer, in the trade since 1978; his firm, Rich Green Design, wires Silicon Valley homes and businesses, and CEDIA gave him its Lifetime Achievement Award in 2024. He chaired the museum’s board for twelve years, unpaid on every filing, through the groundbreaking and the construction, and by the museum’s own telling made sure the restored building was equipped as carefully as it was plastered. In 2025 he received PAHA’s Steve Staiger Award and the Tall Tree Award, then moved back to his hometown, Oshkosh, Wisconsin, staying on as an advisor.

By 2019, a Santa Clara County grant put real numbers on the record: $102,992 for roof repairs, alongside a claim the group had already raised 92 percent of its $9.2 million goal from more than 900 individual donors. Laura Bajuk was signing her name as executive director that year.

LBLaura Bajukexecutive director · 2018 to 2021
A museum professional by trade: a UCLA fine-art degree, San Francisco State’s museum studies program, and eighteen years running the Los Altos History Museum and the Museums of Los Gatos. She appears as the History Museum’s interim director in February 2018 and executive director by that November, and she steered the group through the county grant of 2019 and the city’s hard questions of 2020, disputing the outside reviewers’ read on its pledged money. The museum’s filings list her through the year ending June 2021.
Sources: Museum newsletter, Feb 2018 · Palo Alto Online, 2019 · Palo Alto Online, 2020 · ProPublica Form 990s · Legacy staff bio

Then, in the spring of 2020, the city came close to giving up. The council voted 5 to 1 to invite other organizations to propose different uses for the building. An outside financial review found the group still $2.4 to $2.8 million short, with much of its pledged money carrying what the reviewers called high risk. By December, the choice on the table had narrowed to two: a $6 million "cold shell" that would only stabilize the building for earthquakes, or a full $10.5 million buildout. The council voted 5 to 2 to explore a wider menu of options, including, for the first time on record, an outright sale, with the nonprofit given first right to buy it back if the city went that way. Council member Alison Cormack had toured the building and found medical equipment still sitting there from 2000. She said she was stunned it had been sitting for twenty years. That same month, Bajuk signed a public comment as "E.D., Palo Alto Museum," months before it would have been anybody’s job to make the change official.

Somewhere in that same stretch, in the comments under a local news story about the vote, a man who signed himself geoffpaulsen wrote in to say he was Russel Lee’s grandson, and stayed to tell the rest of it. His grandfather, he wrote, had pioneered group medical practice, built one of the country’s first prepaid health plans, sat on a presidential Medicare commission, and met John F. Kennedy three weeks before Dallas to talk about it. All five of his sons and his daughter went on to Stanford Medical School. He wrote it in on his own, unprompted, and it hasn’t been checked beyond his own telling.

2021-2023

Congress helped, eventually. Representative Anna Eshoo secured a $3 million federal grant for the building in 2021, one piece of an $11.2 million package for her district that cycle, signed into law the following March. That November, the council approved a new forty-year lease at a dollar a year. Cost by then: about $13 million. One more approval document, a construction letter, was expected early the following year.

It came April 17, 2023, approved 6 to 1, Greg Tanaka the lone no. On July 1, they broke ground. Vance Brown Builders sent Tim Stitt. The city sent Assistant City Manager Kiely Nose and Mayor Lydia Kou. Two former mayors, Tom DuBois and Karen Holman, came back for it. Rich Green, going by "Palo Alto Museum Director" now, was there, alongside the museum’s chief operations officer, Diane Moon. Nine hundred people had given money by that point, and the campaign wasn’t done: another $3 million ask, this one to actually run the place once it opened, was already underway.

KHKaren Holmanboard vice chair · former mayor
Karen Holman came to Palo Alto in 1975 by way of Missouri and a graphic-design career, then moved into historic preservation, serving as the Palo Alto History Museum’s own executive director before twenty years in elected office: Planning Commissioner, City Councilmember, and Mayor. Her own board biography credits her as one of the museum’s founders, the one who first launched the idea of a local history museum. She now serves as the board’s vice chair.

Construction ran through 2024. By August, it was a little past half finished: insulation passed inspection upstairs, a transformer set, waterproofing going in on the second-floor balcony. The cost kept moving depending on who was quoting it that week, somewhere between $12 million and $14 million over the life of the project. Tom DuBois, still on the board, said the lease alone had taken nearly two years to negotiate instead of the ninety days everyone expected, and that the delay alone had added roughly $2 million.

2024-2025

Somewhere that year, a Palo Alto family, Thoits Bros., in town since 1893 and now seven generations deep, agreed to fund a new position: the museum’s first president and CEO. The renovation finished the following spring, at a cost most sources put around $14 million, for a building of roughly 20,000 square feet.

Rich Green, describing access to "an archive of everything ever made by Hewlett-Packard" and a separate warehouse collection from the old Museum of American Heritage, told a student reporter that a fragment of Juana Briones’s original adobe wall was already sitting in the lobby, in his own words one of the oldest artifacts in Northern California. Diane Moon was still chief operations officer. On July 1, 2025, Marguerite Gong Hancock started the job the Thoits gift had funded. In her own words, she stepped into the role as the museum’s first president and CEO. A Palo Alto native, two decades at Stanford, chief innovation officer at the Computer History Museum before this and founder of its Exponential Center, co-chair of the town’s Christmas Crèche exhibit, married to Russell Hancock, who runs a regional planning nonprofit of his own. The museum announced it would open the following spring.

MGHMarguerite Gong Hancockpresident and CEO · from July 1, 2025
A Palo Alto native, a schoolteacher’s and a professor’s kid, BYU and then Harvard. She spent two decades at Stanford on how innovation economies work, editing three Stanford University Press books, including The Silicon Valley Edge, then went to the Computer History Museum as chief innovation officer, founding its Exponential Center. She co-founded, and still co-chairs, the town’s Christmas Crèche exhibit. The board announced her as the museum’s first president and CEO on July 16, 2025; she had started the job two weeks earlier.

2026

Early in January, a storm sent water into the basement, the one holding the collection. Some pieces were damaged outright. The rest were put at risk. This was the same basement whose leak had nearly ended the project in 2007, back when it was empty. Now it was full.

February brought more rain and a second leak, along a different wall. Hancock brought in water specialists, architects, contractors, city staff, and went back through the building’s own record of past intrusions to understand the pattern. In a written note to supporters on the 24th, she put it plainly: the building’s history of water problems is long, the causes are complicated, and the fixes have come in layers over the years, none of them final. A local paper reported the delay two days later, alongside a new promise, a date by the end of March, contingent on the museum and the city agreeing on scope, budget, timeline, and approvals.

That date came and went. No one has said publicly what happened to it. Two new board members, Peter Hébert and Dana Tom, started their terms on July 1. The museum’s own materials now describe an opening in stages, sometime after this summer and into next spring. They don’t name a day.

The water came into the same basement twice, nineteen years apart. Both times, the people who’d spent years on the building decided to keep going. They still are.
